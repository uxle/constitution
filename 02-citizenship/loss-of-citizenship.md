# Section 2.7 — Loss of Citizenship

> **Part 02 — Citizenship and Migration** · Articles 2.7.1 – 2.7.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section regulates the loss of citizenship: voluntary renunciation, revocation only for fraud in acquisition, judicial review, and the prohibition of arbitrary deprivation.*

## Article 2.7.1 — Voluntary Renunciation

1. A citizen may renounce citizenship in the manner provided by law.
2. Renunciation shall not be accepted where it would render the person stateless.

## Article 2.7.2 — Revocation for Fraud

1. Citizenship may be revoked only where it was acquired by fraud, false representation, or concealment of a material fact, and only within a reasonable period after its acquisition.
2. Revocation shall be decided by a competent authority on notice to the person concerned and shall state its reasons.
3. Conduct occurring after acquisition shall not ground revocation; changes of belief, residence, or political loyalty shall never ground revocation.

## Article 2.7.3 — Judicial Review of Deprivation

1. A person affected by a decision to revoke citizenship has the right to full judicial review, including of the facts, the law, and the proportionality of the measure, before an independent court.
2. A decision to revoke shall not take final effect until review has been available; where immediate effect is indispensable, the person retains the right to review without delay.

## Article 2.7.4 — Prohibition of Arbitrary Deprivation

1. Citizenship shall not be taken away as punishment for an offense, on political grounds, or on any collective basis.
2. No measure shall render a person stateless, in accordance with Article 2.1.4 (Prevention of Statelessness).
3. A measure affecting a parent shall not of itself affect the citizenship of the parent's child.

## Design Note

*The Section separates voluntary renunciation from revocation and confines revocation to fraud in acquisition within a reasonable period, avoiding the open-ended deprivation grounds that recent comparative practice has shown to be both divisive and error-prone. The absolute bar on statelessness reflects the international treaties on statelessness and treats citizenship as the precondition for exercising every other right. Punitive and collective deprivation are excluded outright because citizenship is a legal status, not an instrument of discipline.*

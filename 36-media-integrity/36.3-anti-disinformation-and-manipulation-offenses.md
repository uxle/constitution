# Section 36.3 — Anti-Disinformation and Manipulation Offenses

## Articles

**Article 36.3.1 (Narrow Definition).** "Disinformation offense" means the knowing, deliberate fabrication or distortion of material facts, spread at scale, for the purpose of manipulating an election, financial market, or public-health decision — never mere error, satire, opinion, or good-faith reporting later found incorrect.

**Article 36.3.2 (No General Speech Crime).** This Article creates no general offense of "false speech"; it is narrowly limited to the deliberate, large-scale manipulation defined in 36.3.1, to prevent it from being misused to suppress dissent or criticism.

**Article 36.3.3 (Platform Transparency Duty).** Large digital platforms must disclose paid political advertising and algorithmic amplification of political content, enforced under Part 17 (Digital Law) and Part 40 (Technology Extended).

*Design Note: The line drawn in 36.3.1–36.3.2 is the most important one in this section — nearly every historical abuse of "anti-disinformation" law has come from blurring the line between genuine manipulation and unwelcome truth or opinion; this Article is written to make that blurring textually difficult.*

**Cross-references:** 1.2 (Freedom of Expression), 17-digital-law, 40-technology-and-future-extended


## Implementation Detail

**Article 36.3.4 (Adjudication Standard).** A disinformation-offense finding under Article 36.3.1 requires proof, to the criminal standard, of both the factual falsity and the deliberate intent to manipulate; a civil regulator may never make this finding — only a court, with full appeal rights.

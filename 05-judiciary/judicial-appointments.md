# Section 5.5 — The Judicial Service Commission

> **Part 05 — The Judiciary** · Articles 5.5.1 – 5.5.5 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section establishes the Judicial Service Commission, sets merit criteria and transparent selection, and makes its recommendations binding on the President.*

## Article 5.5.1 — Composition

1. The Judicial Service Commission shall be composed of a majority of serving judges, together with members of the legal profession, teachers of law, and public members appointed with cross-party support.
2. The composition and the terms of its members shall be fixed by law.
3. The Commission shall be chaired by the President of the Supreme Court.

## Article 5.5.2 — Merit Criteria

1. Appointment shall be by merit, measured by professional excellence, integrity, independence, and suitability for judicial office.
2. Vacancies shall be publicly advertised, with the criteria fixed in advance.
3. The Commission shall seek a judiciary that reflects the diversity of the people of the Republic.

## Article 5.5.3 — Transparent Hearings

1. Candidates shall be interviewed by the Commission.
2. Interviews shall be public, save where the private life of a candidate must be protected.
3. Decisions shall be reasoned and shall be communicated to the candidates.

## Article 5.5.4 — Binding Recommendations

1. For every judicial vacancy, the Commission shall recommend to the President the candidate it considers best qualified.
2. The President shall appoint the person recommended.
3. The President may decline an appointment only where the candidate lacks an eligibility requirement fixed by law, and shall state the reasons in writing.

## Article 5.5.5 — Tenure and Promotion

1. The Commission shall govern the promotion, assignment, and evaluation of judges by published criteria.
2. The law may provide for a probationary first appointment of limited duration.
3. Nothing in this Article limits the removal procedure of Article 5.1.5 (Removal for Cause).

## Design Note

*Making the recommendations binding strips the President of patronage while leaving the formal appointment in the hands of the head of state — stricter in effect than the advisory commissions of the United Kingdom and closer to the professional councils of continental Europe. Public, criteria-based hearings are the cheapest known prophylactic against quiet favouritism. Judicial majorities on the Commission protect independence, while the non-judge members prevent corporate self-selection.*

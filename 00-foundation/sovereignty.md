# Section 0.3 — Sovereignty

> **Part 00 — Foundation** · Articles 0.3.1 – 0.3.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section vests sovereignty in the people of the Republic and regulates how it may be exercised and by whom.*

## Article 0.3.1 — Inalienability of Popular Sovereignty

1. Sovereignty belongs to the people of the Republic, who exercise it in the manner this Constitution provides.
2. Popular sovereignty is inalienable and indivisible; no person, party, or organ may claim it for itself alone.
3. The people act as one body politic under this Constitution, without distinction among citizens of any kind.

## Article 0.3.2 — Exercise of Sovereignty

1. The people exercise their sovereignty through elections and referendums, through the Parliament, the President, the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, the courts, and the institutions of local government.
2. Each of these institutions holds its authority from this Constitution and shall exercise it within the limits this Constitution sets.
3. An exercise of public power without constitutional or statutory authority is invalid from the moment it occurs.

## Article 0.3.3 — Derivation of Public Authority

1. Public authority derives solely from this Constitution and may be created, transferred, or terminated only as this Constitution or a law adopted under it provides.
2. No organ of the State may confer public authority on any person or body except as this Constitution permits.
3. A person acting under color of public power bears the burden of showing the legal basis of that power.

## Article 0.3.4 — Prohibition of Usurpation

1. The seizure or usurpation of public power is unlawful and shall have no legal effect.
2. No lapse of time, election, or acquiescence shall validate an act of usurpation.
3. Obedience to an order of a usurping authority shall not excuse the violation of rights or of this Constitution.

## Design Note

*This Section adapts the classic declaration that sovereignty resides in the nation, stated most influentially in Article 3 of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, to a constitutional democracy in which the people bind themselves through the very constitution by which they act. The trade-off is that sovereignty is described as supreme yet self-limited, so no institution, including a transient parliamentary majority, can claim an unmediated mandate. The anti-usurpation article follows the practice of constitutions that refuse legal validation to coups regardless of their duration or apparent success.*

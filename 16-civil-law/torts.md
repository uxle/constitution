# Section 16.2 — Torts

> **Part 16 — Civil Law** · Articles 16.2.1 – 16.2.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section allocates the losses that private conduct causes: fault liability, strict and vicarious liability for dangerous activities and defective products, protection of personality rights, and the principles of damages.*

## Article 16.2.1 — Fault Liability

1. A person who by intent or negligence causes unlawful damage to another shall make it good.
2. Fault shall be assessed according to the care that could reasonably be required in the circumstances, and liability may be apportioned according to the contribution of each party.

## Article 16.2.2 — Strict and Vicarious Liability

1. A person who operates a dangerous activity, including dangerous structures, animals, emissions, and hazardous substances, shall be liable for the resulting damage even without fault, as provided by law.
2. An employer or principal shall be liable for damage caused by a person acting in the service of, or under the direction of, that employer or principal.
3. The law shall give persons injured by defective products an effective claim independent of contract.

## Article 16.2.3 — Personality Rights

1. Unlawful interference with life, health, dignity, honor, privacy, or image entitles the person affected to injunctive relief and to damages.
2. Remedies shall be applied with regard to the freedom of expression, consistent with Article 1.3.4 (Prohibition of Prior Restraint).

## Article 16.2.4 — Principles of Damages

1. Damages shall restore the injured person so far as money can, covering the loss suffered and the gain prevented.
2. Non-pecuniary loss may be compensated where its gravity warrants.
3. Conduct contributing to the loss on the side of the injured person shall reduce the award, and reasonable mitigation of loss shall be expected.

## Design Note

*A single fault rule keeps the general law simple, while express strict liability for dangerous activities — the modern descendant of the common-law case of Rylands v. Fletcher — carries the losses that enterprise imposes on those who never assumed them; the parallel strict liability of environmental law in Article 13.7.3 confirms the same choice in a neighboring field. Vicarious liability is stated as a general rule so victims do not depend on the solvency of the individual employee. Personality rights receive injunctive relief first, because money cannot un-publish a falsehood, while the express deference to expression safeguards keeps reputation remedies from becoming censorship.*

# Section 16.3 — Property

> **Part 16 — Civil Law** · Articles 16.3.1 – 16.3.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section states the private law of property: ownership with its co-operative forms, the land registry with its protective conclusiveness, good-faith acquisition of movables, and the public-law limits that keep ownership social.*

## Article 16.3.1 — Ownership and Limited Rights

1. Ownership entitles the holder, within the limits of this Constitution and the law, to possess, use, and dispose of the thing, consistent with Article 1.8.1 (Right to Property).
2. Ownership may be held in co-ownership; the shares and the management of co-owned property may be regulated by agreement or by law.
3. Land may be burdened with servitudes and other limited rights in favor of other parcels or of persons, as provided by law.

## Article 16.3.2 — The Land Registry

1. The State shall maintain a public land registry recording ownership and encumbrances of land.
2. A person who acquires a right in land in good faith, for value, and in reliance on the registry shall be protected; the registry shall be conclusive in favor of such an acquirer.
3. Entries shall be made without undue delay, and the refusal or unwarranted delay of an entry shall be reviewable before a court.

## Article 16.3.3 — Good-Faith Acquisition of Movables

1. A person who acquires a movable in good faith, for value, from a person without power of disposal acquires ownership, subject to the exceptions provided by law, including for stolen objects.
2. Protection shall not extend to an acquirer who knew or, through gross negligence, could not have been unaware of the lack of power of disposal.

## Article 16.3.4 — Public-Law Limits

1. Expropriation and restrictions on use are governed by Article 1.8.2 (Expropriation) and Article 1.8.3 (Restrictions on Use).
2. Planning, environmental, and heritage duties attach to ownership as provided by law, and compensation follows the standards of Article 1.8.4 (Compensation and Review).

## Design Note

*The registry rule adopts the conclusiveness principle known from the German Grundbuch and from the Torrens systems of land registration: the register, not the chain of old deeds, is the source of certainty, and the good-faith acquirer for value is protected so that commerce in land can proceed on public faith in the record. Movables follow the opposite and older rule that no one can transfer a better title than one has, softened by the good-faith-purchase exception and the stolen-goods carve-out that most European codes retain. Public-law limits are imported by cross-reference rather than restated, keeping private and constitutional property law aligned.*

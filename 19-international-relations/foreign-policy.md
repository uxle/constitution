# Section 19.1 — Foreign Policy

> **Part 19 — International Relations** · Articles 19.1.1 – 19.1.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section sets the purposes of the Republic's foreign policy, commits it to development cooperation, and subjects the conduct of foreign relations to democratic accountability.*

## Article 19.1.1 — Purposes of Foreign Policy

1. Foreign policy shall serve peace, the security of the Republic, respect for human rights, and fidelity to international law.
2. The Republic shall settle its international disputes by peaceful means and may support collective security arrangements consistent with this Constitution.
3. The Republic shall work for the reduction and control of armaments by international agreement.

## Article 19.1.2 — Development Cooperation

1. The Republic shall conduct development cooperation to reduce poverty and to strengthen human rights, good governance, and sustainability.
2. Cooperation programs shall be transparent, conducted with respect for the priorities of partner peoples, and evaluated for effectiveness.
3. Partner countries and programs shall be selected on published criteria and reported to the Parliament.

## Article 19.1.3 — Democratic Accountability of Foreign Policy

1. The executive shall keep the Parliament currently informed of the substance and development of foreign policy.
2. Commitments of major political or financial significance shall be debated in the Parliament before they bind the Republic.
3. Secrecy in foreign affairs is exceptional, shall be justified in the manner provided by law, and shall be limited in time.

## Article 19.1.4 — Good-Faith Fulfillment of Obligations

1. The Republic shall fulfill its international obligations in good faith.
2. Every organ of the State shall act consistently with the international responsibilities of the Republic and shall support the peaceful cooperation of peoples.

## Design Note

*Purpose clauses of this kind draw on constitutions that place peace and human rights at the head of foreign policy; they are normative enough to be invoked in interpretation without dictating individual diplomatic decisions. The accountability clauses respond to the classic problem that foreign relations are conducted by a small executive out of public view, by requiring information and debate before binding commitments. The good-faith clause makes pacta sunt servanda an internal constitutional duty of every organ, not merely an external obligation of the State.*

# Section 19.6 — Extradition

> **Part 19 — International Relations** · Articles 19.6.1 – 19.6.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section states the conditions of extradition, entrenches mandatory bars for political offenses and serious rights risks, and protects citizens and fair procedure.*

## Article 19.6.1 — Requirements of Extradition

1. Extradition may be granted only on the basis of a treaty or of law, for an act that is criminal under the law of the Republic and of the requesting State, and only upon the decision of a court after the person has had an opportunity to be heard.
2. The executive shall decide on surrender only in conformity with the judicial decision.

## Article 19.6.2 — Mandatory Bars

1. Extradition shall be refused where the offense is a political offense, or where the request seeks to prosecute or punish a person on account of political opinion, religion, nationality, language, or membership of a group, consistent with Article 1.2.2.
2. Extradition shall be refused where there is a real risk of the death penalty or of torture or other cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment; this bar does not depend on the receipt of assurances.
3. Extradition shall be refused where it would be incompatible with any other fundamental right of the person under this Constitution.

## Article 19.6.3 — Citizens

1. A citizen of the Republic shall not be extradited save under a treaty providing for reciprocity that guarantees a fair trial before an independent court, consistent with Article 1.7.1.
2. No citizen shall be deported, expelled, or banished from the territory of the Republic.

## Article 19.6.4 — Procedure and Detention

1. A person whose extradition is sought shall have access to counsel, to interpretation, and to an effective remedy against every adverse decision.
2. Detention pending extradition shall be for the shortest time necessary, subject to periodic judicial review under Article 1.7.4, and shall not be punitive.

## Design Note

*Extradition is surrender to a foreign system of justice, so the safeguards here are procedural and substantive at once: judicial decision, dual criminality, and the classic political-offense bar, which has narrowed in modern practice but survives for persecution dressed as prosecution. The death-penalty and torture bars are made absolute rather than assurance-based because some risks are not cured by diplomatic promises, a position reflected in leading international judgments on surrender. The citizen clause balances the traditional no-extradition rule with treaty reciprocity, always under fair-trial guarantees.*

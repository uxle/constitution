# Section 19.3 — International Law

> **Part 19 — International Relations** · Articles 19.3.1 – 19.3.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section integrates international law into the legal order of the Republic: generally recognized rules bind, peremptory norms are supreme, and all law is interpreted consistently with the Republic's obligations.*

## Article 19.3.1 — Binding Force of International Law

1. The generally recognized rules and principles of international law form part of the law of the Republic.
2. They prevail over conflicting ordinary legislation, subject to this Constitution.

## Article 19.3.2 — Primacy of Peremptory Norms

1. The peremptory norms of general international law (jus cogens) are supreme within the legal order of the Republic.
2. No treaty, custom, agreement, or provision of domestic law may derogate from a peremptory norm; conflicting acts are void.
3. Such norms include, without limitation, the prohibitions of aggression, genocide, slavery, racial discrimination, apartheid, and torture, and the fundamental rules of international humanitarian law.

## Article 19.3.3 — Consistent Interpretation

1. Courts and every public authority shall interpret and apply the law of the Republic consistently with the international obligations of the Republic.
2. This Article applies subject to the supremacy of this Constitution.
3. Judgments and decisions of international courts binding upon the Republic shall be given effect by the competent organs.

## Article 19.3.4 — Implementation of Obligations

1. The Parliament shall enact the legislation necessary to implement binding obligations of the Republic within a reasonable time.
2. The executive shall report periodically to the Parliament on compliance with judgments and decisions of international courts and supervisory bodies.
3. The Republic shall support the peaceful settlement of disputes and the progressive development and codification of international law.

## Design Note

*The clause structure follows the widely adopted monist opening: customary international law enters the domestic order directly and outranks statutes, while the Constitution remains supreme. Declaring jus cogens supreme, as the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties does internationally, prevents any organ of the Republic from lawfully committing the gravest violations even by treaty. The consistent-interpretation duty lets courts harmonize statutes with obligations short of invalidation, and the implementation clause supplies the parliamentary follow-through that monism still requires for treaty law.*

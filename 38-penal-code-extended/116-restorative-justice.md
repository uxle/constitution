# WC-116 · Restorative Justice

> **Part 38 — Penal Code Extended** · Source: World Constitution v1.0 corpus, document 116/300 (adapted) · This edition v1.0.1
> [README](../README.md) · [Glossary](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Subject.** Repair as justice: conferencing, agreements and safeguards

## Preamble

This document institutionalises restorative processes as a mainstream track, with safeguards, not as an alternative experiment.

## Articles

### Article 1 — Availability

1.1 Restorative process is available at every stage: diversion before charge, during trial, before sentencing, and post-sentence, for any offence where victim, offender and prosecutor consent.

1.2 Participation is voluntary for all parties, revocable at any time without penalty.

1.3 Facilitators are trained, certified and independent of the prosecution.

### Article 2 — The process

2.1 Conferencing includes victim, offender, supporters and community delegates; it addresses harm, responsibility and reparation.

2.2 Agreements may include apology, restoration, compensation, service to the victim's choice of community, and treatment.

2.3 Agreements are filed with the court, monitored, and completion cancels or reduces the charge or sentence as agreed.

### Article 3 — Safeguards

3.1 No restorative process without full information, legal advice, and separate preparation of the parties.

3.2 Children participate with guardians and specialists, following document 122 rules.

3.3 Failure or breach returns the case to the ordinary track without the process being used as evidence of guilt or as punishment.

### Article 4 — Serious cases

4.1 In grave cases, restorative process supplements but never replaces the criminal proceeding.

4.2 Victim-led models for organised or sexual violence require specialist accreditation and court approval.

4.3 Community panels may take regulatory and minor Class M matters as a formal diversion, with published outcomes.

## Commentary

The evidence from New Zealand family-group conferencing, Rwanda's post-genocide processes and theelfast experiments is consistent: restorative tracks reduce recidivism, raise victim satisfaction and cost a fraction of custody. The design keeps them mainstream but safe: consent, preparation, advice, and the right to return to the ordinary track. Supplement-not-substitute for grave cases answers the legitimate fear that repair language could swallow justice for violence.

# Design Principles

*About this research note: this note documents the drafting principles actually used in this project — clarity, enforceability, a single limitation clause, tiered amendment, schedules for operational detail, and design notes for pedagogy — and sets each against real drafting practice. It explains the text rather than adding to it; the binding text is the Constitution itself.*

## Clarity

The Constitution is written in short sentences, with one normative idea per clause and the operative verb carrying the rule: "shall" for duties, "may" for powers, "shall not" for prohibitions. The principle has a real drafting pedigree. The United Kingdom's Renton Committee on the Preparation of Legislation (1975) attacked over-complex statutory English; the plain-language movement that followed reshaped public drafting in many jurisdictions, culminating in instruments such as the United States Plain Writing Act 2010. Constitutions written late in the twentieth century — South Africa (1996), Kenya (2010) — read visibly more plainly than their nineteenth-century relatives. This project applies the same discipline structurally as well as verbally: article short titles ("Limitation of Rights", "Prohibition of Monetary Financing") act as headers for retrieval, and every cross-reference carries the article number plus its short title, so a citation can be checked without leaving the page.

## Enforceability

Every norm is drafted so that a court could apply it. Where the text creates a right, it names the organ, the standard, and the remedy: the right to a fair trial (Article 1.7.1) is secured by habeas corpus (Article 1.7.4) and individual complaint after exhaustion of remedies (Article 5.3.4); the audit function names who audits what and who reports to whom (Articles 8.8.1 and 8.8.4). The contrast is with directive language — India's directive principles of state policy and Ireland's directive social principles are famous and deliberately non-justiciable. This project instead follows the South African choice of justiciable social rights, softened by internal qualification: progressive realization and minimum core obligations in healthcare (Articles 1.11.3 and 1.11.4) make the right enforceable against arbitrary retreat while honest about capacity. A norm this Constitution cannot imagine anyone violating — "the State shall be prosperous" — would not belong in it.

## The single limitation clause

All limitation runs through one clause. Article 1.1.4 requires a law of general application and a five-element test — legality, legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, proportionality stricto sensu — with the burden of justification on the State. The design follows Canada's Section 1 and South Africa's Section 36 rather than the per-right qualified clauses of the European Convention on Human Rights, because one transparent test is easier to teach, apply, and audit than a dozen differently worded ones. The counterpart is the closed non-derogable list of Article 1.1.5: a limitation clause this strong is tolerable only if the core — life, physical integrity, slavery, conscience and religion, fair trial, criminal legality — is declared beyond suspension, with any purporting suspension void from adoption.

## Tiered amendment

The Constitution prices change by importance. Ordinary amendments need two-thirds of each House; entrenched provisions (Section 30.5) need that majority plus a national referendum; schedule matters adjust by simple majority (Section 25.1). The tiers echo real practice: the German eternity clause entrenches identity absolutely; South Africa's Section 74 sets higher bars for foundational provisions; Japan's Article 96 requires a referendum; Switzerland submits constitutional amendments to the people routinely. The project's addition is explicit coupling of the tiers to a history record: every adopted change must be entered in the amendment history annex, so the reader can always tell original text from amendment.

## Schedules for operational detail

Oaths, boundary principles, a table of constitutional offices, transition rules, and the protected-provisions list live in Part 30 rather than in the normative Parts. The division copies the function of the Indian and South African schedules: keep the durable rules in the body and the adjustable machinery at the edge, where amendment is cheaper and failure less visible. A text that needed a referendum to change an oath form would be mispriced; a text that kept rights in a schedule would be misbuilt.

## Design notes for pedagogy

Every section ends with a Design Note of two to four sentences that states the rationale and the trade-off struck, and comparative references to real constitutions appear only there — never in the normative text, which speaks for a fictional Republic. The practice serves the project's audience of educators and students: the note converts a drafting choice into a teachable one, and confines real-world citation to the place where it informs rather than distracts. Combined with the annexes (definitions, indexes, cross-reference guide) and these research notes, the corpus is designed to be read linearly by a learner and randomly by a drafter, with the numbering scheme — Part.Section.Article — reliable under both approaches.

# Comparative Electoral Systems

*About this research note: this note surveys the three great families of electoral systems, the working of thresholds and list types, and the indirect election of second chambers, before explaining this project's choice of proportional representation with open lists and a 3% national threshold. It is descriptive and educational; the binding text is Part 06.*

## The three families

Electoral systems translate votes into seats, and every formula buys something and sells something. **Majoritarian** systems — first past the post in the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and India; the two-round system in France; preferential ballots in Australia's lower house — concentrate power and anchor members in districts, but systematically overreward pluralities and can manufacture majorities. **Proportional** systems — list PR across most of continental Europe, the single transferable vote in Ireland and Malta — mirror the electorate's diversity, at the cost of coalition government. **Mixed** systems blend the two: mixed-member proportional (Germany, New Zealand) tops up district winners from party lists so the total result stays proportional, while parallel or non-compensated systems (Japan) seat district winners and list members side by side, leaving the list half only weakly corrective.

| System | Example jurisdictions | Key features | Typical effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First past the post | United Kingdom; United States; Canada; India | One member per district; plurality wins | Strong two-party or two-bloc pressure; disproportional outcomes |
| Two-round majority | France | Runoff between leading candidates when no majority | Encourages alliances; high districting sensitivity |
| Single transferable vote | Ireland; Malta; Australian Senate | Ranked ballots in multimember districts; surplus transfers | Proportional with personal choice; complex counting |
| Closed-list proportional | Spain; Portugal | Parties fix list order; seats filled top-down | Strong proportionality; high party control over candidates |
| Open-list proportional | Finland; Brazil | Voters choose candidates within lists | Proportionality with personal accountability; strong intraparty competition |
| Mixed-member proportional | Germany; New Zealand | District seats plus compensatory list seats | Proportional totals with local representation |
| Parallel mixed (non-compensated) | Japan | District and list seats counted separately | Partial proportionality; large-party advantage persists |

## Thresholds and list types

Thresholds are the counterweight that keeps parliaments governable. Germany and New Zealand exclude parties below 5% (with a seat-holding escape valve in New Zealand); Turkey long excluded parties below 10%, recently relaxed; Israel lowers its bar to 3.25%; the Netherlands' pure PR carries only the arithmetic threshold of one seat in a 150-member chamber, about 0.67%. The trade-off is exact: a 1% threshold lets small ideological families win representation but fragments coalitions; a 10% threshold stabilizes cabinets but silences whole electorates. Open versus closed lists shift power inside parties: open lists (Finland, Brazil) make candidates answer to voters and weaken party machines; closed lists (Spain, Portugal) let parties present balanced teams, including through zipper lists that alternate by gender, at the price of candidate selection controlled from the top.

This project sits deliberately in the middle of each axis. Article 6.1.2 gives the Lower House proportional representation with **open candidate lists** and a **3% national threshold**, allocated by the **Droop quota** over province-wide lists (Article 6.1.3). The 3% line excludes micro-parties without extinguishing new movements; open lists preserve the personal accountability that pure closed lists surrender; the Droop quota, shared with the single transferable vote, allocates seats with the least wasted vote of the standard formulas. Article 6.1.1 states the underlying principle — formal equality of the vote — and Article 6.1.4 ties district boundaries to an independent commission under the principles of Section 30.2, because thresholds and formulas matter little if boundary lines are drawn by the incumbents they entrench. Article 6.1.5 then stabilizes electoral law for a fixed period, echoing the comparative lesson that rules must not be rewritten by their expected beneficiaries between elections.

## Second chambers and election administration

The Upper House is elected indirectly: three members per Province, chosen by the Provincial Councils for staggered six-year terms (Section 3.3). Indirect election has real precedents — India's Council of States is elected by state legislative assemblies; Ireland's Senate mixes vocational panels with university seats — while Canada's appointed chamber and Germany's Bundesrat of state governments show the appointed and executive alternatives. The indirect route fits this project's unitary design: the chamber represents Provinces as territorial units without duplicating a direct popular mandate, and its powers are confined to delay (Articles 3.3.4 and 3.3.5).

Administration is constitutionalized as well. Universal suffrage begins at 18; registration is automatic (Article 6.2.1); the ballot is secret and accessible (Articles 6.3.1 and 6.3.2); and paper ballots or a verified paper trail are mandatory everywhere, with post-election audits (Article 6.4.1 and 6.4.4) — a response to the recurring comparative finding that trust in outcomes depends less on the formula than on visible, verifiable administration by an independent commission (Sections 3.5 and 20.4). Disputes run to the Constitutional Court on expedited timelines (Article 6.6.2), closing the loop between electoral design and constitutional adjudication.

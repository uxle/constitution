# Comparative Economic Systems

*About this research note: this note surveys how real systems protect central bank independence, discipline public finance with fiscal rules and fiscal councils, and govern public ownership, before explaining this project's design in Sections 8.4 and 8.7. It is descriptive and educational; the binding text is Part 08.*

## Central bank independence

Modern monetary constitutions separate the goal from the politician. The German Bundesbank, established by its 1957 act, became the template: legal independence, a price-stability orientation, and a prohibition on financing the state. The European Union generalized the model — the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union gives the European Central Bank a primary mandate of price stability (Article 127), forbids member-state instruction (Article 130), and prohibits central banks from extending overdraft facilities or buying sovereign debt directly from their governments (Article 123). The United States chose a dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices; New Zealand's Reserve Bank Act 1989 pioneered statutory inflation targeting, later imitated worldwide. Comparative experience draws two distinctions: **goal independence** (who sets the objective) is rare, while **instrument independence** (how the objective is pursued) is the global norm; and independence is always purchased with accountability — published minutes, reports to the legislature, and appearances before parliamentary committees.

This Constitution follows the mainstream. Article 8.4.1 gives the Central Bank independence with a primary mandate of price stability supporting the general economic policy; Article 8.4.3 reserves note issue to it; Article 8.4.4 equips it with instruments and financial-stability duties; and Article 8.4.5 requires public accountability reporting. The prohibition of monetary financing appears in Article 8.3.4, with the narrow exception allowing ordinary monetary-policy operations on market terms — the same carve-out that the European prohibition contains, because a central bank that may never hold sovereign paper could not conduct open-market operations at all.

## Fiscal rules and fiscal councils

Rules that bind budgets attempt to solve a structural bias: governments spend other people's future money. The German debt brake, written into the Basic Law in 2009, caps the federal structural deficit at 0.35% of gross domestic product; the Swiss debt brake (Article 126 of its 1999 Constitution) requires expenditure to track estimated revenue over the cycle; the European Union's reference values of 3% deficit and 60% debt (Maastricht, 1992) anchor its excessive-deficit procedure. A second family is transparency-led: New Zealand's Fiscal Responsibility Act 1994 forces governments to publish honest projections rather than capping numbers directly; Chile's structural-balance rule since 2001 ties spending to medium-term revenue estimated by independent panels.

Because rules invite creative accounting, a third generation of institutions audits them: the United Kingdom's Office for Budget Responsibility (created by statute in 2011) and Ireland's Fiscal Advisory Council produce or scrutinize independent forecasts, while the long-established Netherlands bureau for economic policy analysis shows how decades of independent projection discipline debate. This Constitution's Article 8.7 states the principle of intergenerational equity (Article 8.7.1), requires deficit and debt reference targets to be set by law (Article 8.7.2) — leaving numbers to ordinary legislation, which can adapt — and establishes an independent fiscal council (Article 8.7.3) with risk-reporting duties (Article 8.7.4). Budgetary process remains parliamentary: the annual budget must be adopted before the fiscal year begins (Article 8.2.3), and no expenditure is lawful without authorization (Article 8.6.1), with every public fund audited by the Office of the Auditor-General (Articles 8.8.1 and 20.5.1).

## Ownership models

Constitutions also decide who owns what. State ownership of enterprises is a fact in nearly every economy; the comparative question is governance. The OECD Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises (2015) codify the emerging standard — competitive neutrality, commercially objective ownership, transparent reporting — so that public firms neither distort markets nor become hidden treasuries. Natural-resource revenues receive constitutional treatment where they dominate the budget: Norway's Government Pension Fund Global converts petroleum wealth into a diversified, rule-governed fund for future generations, and Chile's stabilization funds smooth copper-driven cycles.

The pattern behind the table is consistent: constitutional texts fix principles, institutions, and prohibitions, while statutes fix numbers, rates, and procedures. This Constitution keeps that division. It entrenches independence, prohibits monetization, and requires sustainability, but it does not entrench a deficit percentage, an interest rate, or an ownership percentage — the lessons of the comparative record suggest that rigid numbers age badly, while independent institutions with clear mandates age well.

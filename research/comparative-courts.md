# Comparative Courts

*About this research note: this note compares how real systems organize judicial review of legislation and appoint their highest judges, and then explains this project's choice of a distinct Constitutional Court with binding commission recommendations. It is descriptive and educational; the binding text is Part 05.*

## Centralized versus diffuse review

The organizing question is: who may refuse to apply an unconstitutional law? The **diffuse** model, born with Marbury v. Madison (1803) in the United States, lets every judge in the ordinary hierarchy disregard statutes contrary to the constitution; review is incidental to ordinary litigation. The **centralized** model, invented by Hans Kelsen for the Austrian Constitution of 1920, concentrates the power in a dedicated court, sometimes with exclusive jurisdiction, so that constitutional questions are decided uniformly and, often, in the abstract. Germany, Italy, Spain, and France follow the centralized design with constitutional courts or councils; Japan (Article 81) and Canada follow the American pattern, with review available across the court system.

Both models generate hybrids. India gives the Supreme Court and the High Courts concurrent constitutional jurisdiction (Articles 32 and 226), producing diffuse review with a strong apex court; Brazil combines concrete review by all judges with abstract review concentrated in its Supreme Federal Tribunal. The practical trade-offs concern consistency, access, and speed: diffuse review constitutionalizes all litigation but risks divergence among courts; centralized review is uniform but can bottleneck, which is why Germany and South Africa filter access to their constitutional courts while ordinary courts send preliminary questions upward.

| System | Review model | Apex constitutional organ | Access design |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States (1787) | Diffuse; all courts | Supreme Court of the United States | Cases and controversies only |
| Austria (1920) | Centralized; origin of the model | Constitutional Court | Referral by courts; party and minority access |
| Germany (1949) | Centralized | Federal Constitutional Court | Filtered individual complaints; preliminary referrals |
| Italy (1947) | Centralized | Constitutional Court | Incidental referral by ordinary courts in genuine cases |
| France (1958) | Centralized; ex ante review, later reinforced by a preliminary-ruling mechanism (2008 reform) | Constitutional Council | Referral by litigants through the priority question of constitutionality |
| Canada (1982) | Diffuse; all courts | Supreme Court of Canada | Any proceeding raising the Charter |
| Japan (1947) | Diffuse; all courts (Article 81) | Supreme Court of Japan | Incidental in ordinary litigation |
| India (1950) | Hybrid; diffuse with strong apex | Supreme Court of India | Direct petitions (Article 32) and High Court petitions (Article 226) |
| South Africa (1996) | Hybrid; appeals concentrated at the apex | Constitutional Court | Constitutional matters; leave to appeal filters |
| This Constitution | Hybrid | the Constitutional Court (Section 5.3) | Concrete review by any court (Article 5.6.2); abstract review and individual complaints after exhaustion of remedies centralized (Articles 5.6.3 and 5.3.4) |

This project's design is deliberately hybrid. Every court may refuse to apply an unconstitutional act, so constitutional defense never waits on a single docket (Articles 5.6.1 and 5.6.2); but abstract review, inter-organ and Province disputes, election validity, and individual complaints after ordinary remedies are exhausted belong exclusively to the Constitutional Court (Article 5.3.3). The separation of the Constitutional Court from the Supreme Court (Section 5.2) mirrors the German and South African practice of giving constitutional questions a specialist bench, while the American insight is preserved in the diffuse floor of concrete review.

## Appointment models

Who appoints judges decides whose constitution it is. The United States combines presidential nomination with Senate confirmation and life tenure. Germany elects its Federal Constitutional Court judges in halves by the two legislative chambers, each by a two-thirds majority, for single non-renewable twelve-year terms. Italy splits appointments three ways among the head of state, Parliament, and the supreme courts. France uses nine-year non-renewable terms with appointments shared between the President and the chambers. South Africa interviews candidates publicly through a Judicial Service Commission before the President appoints; Kenya's commission recruits with public participation; India's collegium of senior judges selects, a practice unique in the democratic world.

| Jurisdiction | Appointment method | Term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| United States | President nominates; Senate confirms | Life tenure |
| Germany | Bundestag and Bundesrat each elect half, two-thirds majority | Twelve years, non-renewable |
| Italy | One third President, one third Parliament, one third supreme courts | Nine years, non-renewable |
| South Africa | Judicial Service Commission interviews; President appoints | Twelve years or retirement age |
| Kenya | Judicial Service Commission recruits with public participation; President appoints | Tenure protected by constitution |
| This Constitution | Judicial Service Commission recommends by binding recommendation (Section 5.5); President appoints | Eleven judges; single non-renewable twelve-year terms, staggered (Section 5.3) |

The binding recommendation is this project's distinctive choice. Where a commission merely advises, the appointing organ retains a final political filter; where a collegium monopolizes selection, accountability to other branches thins. Making the Judicial Service Commission's recommendation binding (Article 5.5.4) — after transparent, merit-based hearings (Article 5.5.3) — removes the last discretion from the appointing President while leaving the formal act of appointment in constitutional hands, a compromise between the South African and German models that keeps appointments professional, public, and predictable. The single non-renewable term, with one-third of seats expiring every four years, then protects sitting judges: no one rules on cases knowing that a future reappointment depends on pleasing today's politicians.

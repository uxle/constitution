# Comparative Rights

*About this research note: this note compares how real constitutional systems limit and suspend rights, and how they make rights bind private parties, before explaining the choices made in this project's Part 01. It is descriptive and educational; the binding text of this Constitution is Part 01 itself.*

## Limitation clauses: three designs

Constitutional rights are rarely absolute, and drafters have chosen three ways to say so. The **European Convention on Human Rights** writes a qualified clause into each high-level right: interference must be "in accordance with the law", pursue a listed "legitimate aim", and be "necessary in a democratic society". The approach is precise but repetitive, and courts must harmonize differently worded clauses. **Canada's Charter** (Section 1) and **South Africa's Constitution** (Section 36) invert the design with a single master clause covering all rights; the Canadian Supreme Court's Oakes test (1986) requires a pressing and substantial objective, rational connection, minimal impairment, and proportionality of effects. The **United States Bill of Rights** contains no general limitation clause at all; judges supply the filter through tiers of scrutiny, from rational-basis review to strict scrutiny.

This project adopts the single master clause. Article 1.1.4 (Limitation of Rights) requires a law of general application and a five-element test — legality, legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, and proportionality stricto sensu — and places the burden of justification on the State. Compared with the Convention model, one clause is easier to teach and applies identically to every right; compared with the American model, the test is written down, so its elements are contestable in text rather than discoverable only in doctrine.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| European Convention on Human Rights (1950) | Per-right qualified clauses (Articles 8–11): law, aim, necessity in a democratic society | Article 15, in war or public emergency; Articles 2, 3, 4(1), and 7 non-derogable | Indirect, through Convention obligations on states |
| International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966) | Per-right clauses | Article 4, with formal notification; strictly required by the exigencies of the situation | Limited; states parties bind themselves |
| United States Bill of Rights (1791) | No general clause; judicial scrutiny fills the gap | None expressed; habeas corpus suspension clause in the main text | Weak: the state-action doctrine restricts application to private parties |
| Canadian Charter (1982) | Section 1: reasonable limits, demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society | Sections 32–33: no formal derogation, but a legislative override with a five-year sunset | Section 32: binds governments and legislatures |
| German Basic Law (1949) | Per-right limits by statute; essence of the right inviolable (Article 19(2)); proportionality doctrine developed judicially | None in peacetime; emergency articles removed in 1968 reform | Indirect horizontal effect (Drittwirkung) through general clauses, from the Lüth case (1958) |
| South African Constitution (1996) | Section 36: law of general application; reasonableness and justifiability; less restrictive means considered | Section 37, with parliamentary approval and a fixed non-derogable core | Section 8(2): binds natural and juristic persons where capable |

## Derogation in emergencies

The Convention and the Covenant permit formal derogation in public emergencies, subject to non-derogable cores: the right to life, freedom from torture, freedom from slavery, and legality in criminal law. South Africa's Section 37 adds procedural muscle — parliamentary approval and a defined list that survives the emergency. Canada took the opposite path, preferring a legislative override (Section 33) that operates even in ordinary times but expires after five years. This project accepts derogation machinery nowhere: Article 1.1.5 lists the non-derogable rights — life; freedom from torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment; freedom from slavery and forced labor; conscience and religion; the fair-trial core including habeas corpus; and the principle of legality in criminal law — and declares any purporting suspension void from adoption. The emergency regime of Part 26 therefore regulates measures rather than suspending rights.

## Horizontal effect

Whether rights bind private parties decides a constitution's reach into workplaces, contracts, and platforms. The American state-action doctrine keeps the Bill of Rights aimed at government; the German courts achieved indirect horizontal effect by reading private-law general clauses through fundamental rights, beginning with Lüth (1958); South Africa wrote the point into Section 8(2). This project follows South Africa: Article 1.1.2 binds all organs of the State and also natural and juristic persons in their relations with one another to the extent the right is capable of such application — a formula that lets privacy and expression reach digital platforms without pretending that every private preference is a constitutional violation.

# Comparative Constitutions

*About this research note: this note compares the structure and rigidity of eight real constitutions and relates them to the design of this project's Constitution. It is written for educational use; the comparison is descriptive, and no real constitutional arrangement is endorsed as a model to be transplanted.*

## Eight constitutions, briefly

| Constitution | Form of government | Structure | Amendment rigidity | A lesson for this project |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States (1787) | Presidential republic | Federal; seven brief articles plus 27 amendments | Article V: two-thirds of both chambers plus three-fourths of the states; never comprehensively rewritten | Short texts need strong courts; this project chose more detail with a written limitation clause instead |
| Germany, Basic Law (1949) | Parliamentary republic | Federal; eternity clause protects dignity, democracy, and federalism (Article 79(3)) | Two-thirds of both chambers; core unamendable | Source of the constructive vote of no confidence used in Article 4.2.4 |
| India (1950) | Parliamentary republic | Federal in form with strong central features; the longest national constitution | Article 368 procedures; courts police an unwritten "basic structure" | Shows how a detailed constitution is amended often; this project instead fixes operational detail in Schedules |
| South Africa (1996) | Parliamentary republic | Unitary with provinces; justiciable bill of rights | Tiered under Section 74: higher majorities for foundational Section 1 | Direct ancestor of this project's single limitation clause and tiered amendment design |
| Canada (1867/1982) | Parliamentary democracy under a constitutional monarch | Federal; Charter of Rights added in 1982 | Section 38 general formula (seven provinces with half the population); Section 33 override | The notwithstanding clause warns against override mechanisms; this project has none |
| Japan (1947) | Parliamentary constitutional monarchy | Unitary; symbolic emperor; pacifist Article 9 | Article 96: two-thirds of both chambers plus a majority referendum; never amended | Confirms that a referendum-anchored amendment route can stabilize a text for decades |
| Brazil (1988) | Presidential republic | Federal; detailed social and economic rights | Three-fifths of each chamber in two rounds; some matters unamendable | Illustrates the cost of exhaustive detail: frequent amendment of ordinary matters |
| Kenya (2010) | Presidential republic | Unitary with devolved counties; extensive bill of rights | Parliamentary route and a popular-initiative route, both ending in a referendum | Model for this project's citizens'-initiative referendum route (Section 6.7) |

## What the comparison shows

Three axes organize the eight texts. The first is **form of government**: the United States and Brazil elect a president who is both head of state and head of government, while Germany, India, South Africa, Canada, and Japan locate executive power in a cabinet responsible to the legislature. This project follows the parliamentary family, but with a distinction borrowed from practice rather than from any single text: a President elected by Parliament for a single six-year term as head of state, and a Prime Minister who must hold the confidence of the Lower House. The single non-renewable term avoids the re-election incentives that have strained presidential systems, while the constructive vote of no confidence, taken from the German model, protects against cabinet instability.

The second axis is **territorial structure**: five of the eight are federal in law or in practice, while Japan is unitary and South Africa and Kenya are unitary with substantial devolution. This project adopts the last pattern — a unitary state whose Provinces, Regions, and Municipalities are protected by a subsidiarity principle rather than by divided sovereignty — because it lets the text model devolution without importing the complex disputes that federal allocation of competences produces.

The third axis is **rigidity**. The United States text is nearly immovable in practice; Japan's has never been amended; India's is formally flexible but judicially bounded; South Africa and Germany entrench only their cores. This project's tiers (Section 25.1) sit deliberately in the South African position: ordinary amendments by two-thirds of each House, entrenched provisions (Section 30.5) also requiring a referendum majority, and Schedules adjustable by simple majority. The design keeps the constitutional core where real constitutions keep theirs — dignity, democracy, judicial independence — while admitting that operational detail must remain cheap to adjust.

Finally, the eight texts differ in **detail**: the American Constitution fits in a pamphlet, the Indian fills a volume. This project occupies a middle course — full enough that a student can trace money, emergencies, and digital rights from right to institution to procedure, yet spare enough that the thematic Parts remain readable from beginning to end.

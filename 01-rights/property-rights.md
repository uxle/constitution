# Section 1.8 — Property Rights

> **Part 01 — Rights and Freedoms** · Articles 1.8.1 – 1.8.4 · Adopted — v1.0.0

*This Section protects ownership and inheritance and governs expropriation, restrictions on use, compensation, and judicial review.*

## Article 1.8.1 — Right to Property

1. Every person has the right to own, use, enjoy, and dispose of property lawfully acquired, alone or with others, and to inherit property.
2. No person shall be arbitrarily deprived of property. Intangible and intellectual interests enjoy protection to the extent provided by law.

## Article 1.8.2 — Expropriation

1. Expropriation is permissible only for a public purpose, only by or under a law, and only against just and prompt compensation.
2. The public purpose shall be determined by law and shall not serve primarily the benefit of another private person.
3. Compensation shall reflect the market value of the property at the time of the taking, shall be determined through a transparent procedure, and shall be paid promptly and, where staged, with interest from the date of the taking.

## Article 1.8.3 — Restrictions on Use

1. The use of property may be regulated by law for reasons of public health, safety, the environment, planning, and heritage, subject to Article 1.1.4 (Limitation of Rights).
2. Regulation that imposes on a particular owner a disproportionate individualized burden equivalent in effect to a taking shall be treated as expropriation and compensated accordingly.

## Article 1.8.4 — Compensation and Review

1. A person whose property is taken or restricted enjoys judicial review of the taking, of the restriction, and of the amount of compensation.
2. Review shall be accessible and expeditious, and compensation procedures shall not be so slow or uncertain as to nullify the guarantee.
3. The detailed rules of ownership and succession between private persons are stated in Part 16 (Civil Law).

## Design Note

*The classical triad — public purpose, legal basis, compensation — is completed by judicial review and by the rule on disguised expropriation, which prevents the State from achieving through regulation what it could not achieve through formal taking. The market-value benchmark and the requirement of prompt compensation reflect the standards found in international investment law and in modern constitutional texts. Property is protected as an institution of personal freedom rather than as an absolute barrier to land policy, which the affirmative-measures clause of Article 1.2.5 (Affirmative Measures) and planning law mediate.*
